# Physics 101 Chapters 2-3: Curated Content And Storyboards

Prepared: 18 August 2026

## Curriculum Basis

- AC9S4U03 asks Year 4 students to identify forces exerted by one object on another and investigate how frictional, gravitational and magnetic forces affect motion.
- AC9S4U03 elaborations explicitly include friction causing objects to slow and force arrows representing force direction and relative magnitude.
- AC9S4I01-I06 support prediction, fair tests, formal observations, tables, simple column graphs, evaluation and evidence-based communication.
- Source: [Australian Curriculum F-6 Science elements](https://www.australiancurriculum.edu.au/media/7158/science_all_elements_f-6.pdf)
- Source: [ACARA teacher background information for AC9S4U03](https://v9.australiancurriculum.edu.au/support-resources/background-information/science_teacher_background_information_AC9S4U03_E5)

## Shared Production Rules

- Audience: an able Grade 4 learner, approximately 8-10 years old.
- Duration target: 195-220 seconds, matching the approved Chapter 1 production rhythm.
- Voice: warm Australian primary science teacher; precise, conversational, and curious.
- Visual changes: one meaningful change for every caption cue; no narration stretch beyond eight seconds without a new event, comparison, reveal, measurement or intentional prediction hold.
- Emphasis: borders, arrows and callouts remain until the spoken referent ends.
- Force arrows: labelled `object on object`; direction is explicit; relative length is compared only within a scene.
- Scope: no Newton units, acceleration formula, formal mass-weight distinction or coefficient of friction.

## Chapter 2: Motion Tells The Story

### Learning target

Use before-and-after observations to identify starting, speeding up, slowing down, stopping and changing direction. Explain that a kick acts during contact; it is not stored inside the moving ball. Introduce surface friction only as the contact interaction that helps slow the ball, leaving useful/harmful friction and detailed surface investigations to Chapter 4.

### Hero frame

Three identical glowing test lanes share a smooth launch section and an equal-speed timing gate before the tile, short-grass and thick-carpet test surfaces. Distance tracks remain visible. The launch arrow vanishes after contact, while a backward `surface resistance` arrow identifies the carpet's friction-and-deformation effect during the slow-down comparison.

### Narration and visual beats

1. **Three tracks, one mystery**
   - Narration: "Three identical balls. Three different tracks. Each ball gets the same launch. Tile, short grass, or thick carpet: which ball will travel furthest before it stops? Make your prediction, then watch for evidence."
   - Visual: orient all three lanes; lock identical ball and start markers; reveal the three surface labels; hold prediction cover.

2. **Run the comparison**
   - Narration: "All three begin still. The same launcher setting sends each ball across a smooth launch strip. A timing gate confirms the same entry speed before each test surface. Now the launcher is clear. Tile gives the longest stopping distance. Grass is shorter. Carpet is shortest."
   - Visual: simultaneous launch; contact arrows appear only during contact and disappear together; equal-speed gates flash before the surfaces; animated position traces show three stopping distances.

3. **Name the motion changes**
   - Narration: "Motion can tell several stories. Still to moving means start. More distance in the same time means speed up. Less distance each moment means slow down. No new distance means stop. A new path means change direction."
   - Visual: five compact motion strips animate in sequence with persistent labels: start, speed up, slow down, stop, turn.

4. **Before and after evidence**
   - Narration: "A physicist compares before and after. Before the launch, the carpet ball is still. Just after contact, it is moving. Later, its gaps become smaller. Finally, its position no longer changes. That sequence is measurable evidence."
   - Visual: time-stamped carpet lane snapshots at 0, 1, 2 and 3 seconds; spacing brackets shrink; final stop marker locks.

5. **The kick has ended**
   - Narration: "Look closely at the contact. While the launcher touches the ball, launcher on ball is a forward push. Once the launcher separates, that contact force ends. The ball can still be moving even though the kick is over."
   - Visual: zoom on launcher-ball contact; forward arrow and contact ring; clean separation; arrow removed while a motion trail continues.

6. **What slows the ball?**
   - Narration: "The moving ball still interacts with its surroundings. The surface touches the ball. In this carpet model, friction and carpet deformation produce a backward surface resistance. The greater slowing effect gives a shorter stopping distance."
   - Visual: magnified ball-surface patch; backward `surface resistance` arrow; carpet fibres deform; compare short and long distance rails.

7. **Build a fair test**
   - Narration: "To compare surfaces fairly, keep the ball, entry speed and start line the same. Change only the surface. Measure from the surface start to the resting ball. Repeat each run, because one surprising run may not represent the pattern."
   - Visual: three locks labelled ball, entry speed and start; surface tile is the only changing card; ruler appears; three repeat dots fill.

8. **Read the data**
   - Narration: "Check all three runs before the averages. Tile measures three hundred and thirty-five, three hundred and forty-five and three hundred and forty centimetres: average three hundred and forty. Grass averages two hundred and twenty. Carpet averages one hundred and ten. The graph and tracks show the same pattern."
   - Visual: individual runs reveal before each average; column graph grows from zero; matching colour traces connect bars to lanes.

9. **Test the tempting explanation**
   - Narration: "A tempting explanation says the kick force rides inside the ball and slowly runs out. But the contact arrow ended when the launcher separated. The remaining evidence shows a ball moving, while surface friction acts against that motion. The kick is not stored inside."
   - Visual: `stored kick` capsule enters ball, receives a red cross; contact timeline replays; friction arrow remains; corrected explanation stamp appears.

10. **Direction is motion evidence too**
   - Narration: "A force can change direction as well as speed. A rolling ball meets an angled bumper. The bumper-on-ball force points away from the bumper surface. That force changes the ball's motion into a new path: straight in, diagonal out."
   - Visual: overhead lane; ball hits angled bumper; force arrow is perpendicular to the bumper face; the separate motion path transforms from straight to diagonal; before/after headings persist.

11. **Make a prediction**
   - Narration: "Suppose we replace thick carpet with a more deformable, higher-resistance rubber mat while everything else stays the same. Predict the stopping distance. Would it be longer than tile, between tile and carpet, or shorter than carpet? Use the model, then test it."
    - Visual: prediction panel with three ordered distance rails; three-second quiet hold; reveal `shorter than carpet` as the evidence-based prediction, labelled `test this next` rather than fact.

12. **Your evidence routine**
    - Narration: "Use this motion routine. Compare before and after. Name the motion change. Name the interacting objects. Then connect the force to visible evidence. The ball does not carry a used-up kick. Motion changes because objects are interacting now, or interacted earlier while other forces continue to act."
    - Visual: four-step rail: compare, name change, name pair, justify; final tile/grass/carpet hero returns with corrected force timeline.

### Accuracy guardrails

- Do not imply that motion requires a continuing forward force.
- Do not leave the launch arrow on screen after launcher-ball contact ends.
- Describe the carpet comparison as evidence from this controlled model, not a universal numerical law. Use `surface resistance` for the net backward rolling-resistance effect.
- Label the rubber-mat outcome as a prediction to test.
- Chapter 4 retains distinct scope: useful grip, reduced friction and surface-design applications rather than repeating this stopping-distance evidence lesson.

## Chapter 3: Push, Pull And Support

### Learning target

Model applied pushes, applied pulls and support forces. Use a rubber-band comparison meter to collect relative stretch readings in centimetres. Explain that a resting surface is an interacting object, not an inactive obstacle.

### Hero frame

A thick book rests on a bright foam support block. The compressed foam is visible beneath the book. First, `book on foam` and `foam on book` appear as an interaction-pair diagram with different receiving objects. It then changes to a forces-on-book model: `Earth on book` down and `foam on book` up. A nearby cart and rubber-band meter provide the measurement transfer.

### Narration and visual beats

1. **The quiet force mystery**
   - Narration: "This book is not moving, but the foam beneath it is squashed. Is the foam doing nothing? Watch the contact patch. The book presses down, and the foam changes shape. What might the foam be doing to the book?"
   - Visual: book lowers onto foam; foam visibly compresses; close-up border remains through the question; prediction marker holds.

2. **Name both objects**
   - Narration: "The book and foam interact through contact. Book on foam pushes down; foam on book pushes up. Those arrows act on different objects. To explain the resting book, follow forces on the book: Earth on book points down, while foam on book support points up."
   - Visual: interaction-pair arrows appear first with two receiver badges; then transform to a forces-on-book model with Earth down and support up.

3. **Still does not mean no forces**
   - Narration: "The book remains still while the foam is supporting it. Remove the foam, and the book begins to fall. Put the support back, and the book can rest again. Stillness is not proof that no forces act."
   - Visual: three-state timeline: supported/resting, support removed/falling, support restored/resting; red cross over `still = no forces`.

4. **Support changes with the load**
   - Narration: "Add a second identical book. The foam squashes further. To support the resting stack, the foam's upward push is greater than it was for one book. A longer arrow shows greater force within this comparison."
   - Visual: second book settles; compression ruler increases; upward cyan arrow lengthens; explicit `compare only in this scene` tag.

5. **Applied pushes**
   - Narration: "Now move to the cart. A robot hand touches the cart and pushes to the right. Hand on cart is an applied force. The arrow begins on the cart and points in the direction of the push."
   - Visual: hand approaches, contact ring appears, cart begins moving; gold force arrow originates on cart; object-pair label persists.

6. **Applied pulls**
   - Narration: "A rubber band can pull the same cart. The band stretches towards the robot. Rubber band on cart points towards the robot too. Push and pull describe the direction of the applied interaction; neither is a material stored in the cart."
   - Visual: band attaches and stretches; pull arrow aligns with band; push and pull comparison labels; stored-force capsule crossed out.

7. **Build a comparison meter**
   - Narration: "We can use band stretch as a comparison meter. Mark the resting length as zero centimetres. Then read how many centimetres the band stretches while pulling. More stretch means a greater pull in this investigation. These are relative readings, not Newtons."
   - Visual: enlarged ruler and band; zero mark; 2 cm and 4 cm examples; `relative comparison` badge; `not Newtons` scope note.

8. **Run a fair pull test**
   - Narration: "Keep the cart, surface and start line the same. First, hold a two-centimetre stretch for one second. Reset. Then hold a four-centimetre stretch for one second. Change only the stretch reading and measure the distance after the same time."
   - Visual: locks for cart/surface/start/time; split trial 2 cm versus 4 cm; timer circles; distance traces stop together.

9. **Read the result**
   - Narration: "At two centimetres, the cart travels eighty centimetres. At four centimetres, it travels one hundred and fifty centimetres. The larger stretch reading goes with the larger change in motion in this test. The data supports a stronger-pull explanation."
   - Visual: two-row table; bars rise to 80 and 150; corresponding gold arrows compare lengths; evidence stamp.

10. **Repair the silent-table idea**
    - Narration: "Now test the claim: a table does nothing because it does not move. Place the foam and book on the table. The contact patch changes shape, and the book rests instead of passing through. The surface is part of the interaction."
    - Visual: claim card; table/foam/book stack; compression close-up; surface highlighted; claim receives red cross and corrected support statement.

11. **Choose the correct model**
   - Narration: "Which model fits the forces acting on a book resting on foam? One downward Earth-on-book arrow only? No arrows because it is still? Or Earth on book down paired with foam on book support up? Choose, then check the contact evidence."
   - Visual: three model cards; three-second quiet hold; wrong models dim; the two-forces-on-book model expands over the actual contact patch.

12. **Your force-model routine**
    - Narration: "Use this routine. Name both objects. Mark the contact. Draw the force on the receiving object. Point the arrow in the force direction. When comparing pulls, use the same meter and a fair test. Even a still surface can push. Evidence makes that invisible interaction understandable."
    - Visual: five-step rail with book/foam and cart/meter examples converging into one final force-model board.

### Accuracy guardrails

- Use `support force`, not formal `normal reaction` terminology.
- Do not claim that a resting object has no forces.
- Do not imply that centimetres are a standard unit of force; they are a relative meter reading for this investigation only.
- Only compare arrow lengths within the same visual setup.
- Keep both forces in the resting-book model anchored to the receiving book. Keep interaction-pair arrows explicitly separated from forces-on-one-object arrows.

## Assessment Blueprint

Each chapter will have 20 questions: two variants in each of ten slots. Every attempt selects one item per slot and rotates options deterministically.

### Chapter 2 slots

1. Identify a motion change from before/after evidence.
2. Interpret shrinking or growing position gaps.
3. Identify when the launch contact force ends.
4. Explain surface friction as an interaction.
5. Compare stopping-distance evidence.
6. Diagnose a fair or unfair surface test.
7. Read the 340/220/110 cm table or graph.
8. Reject the stored-kick misconception.
9. Explain a direction change at a bumper.
10. Build a claim-evidence-reasoning chain in a new context.

### Chapter 3 slots

1. Identify the book-foam interaction pair.
2. Interpret the upward support-force arrow.
3. Reject `still means no forces`.
4. Predict support/compression when a second book is added.
5. Model an applied push on a cart.
6. Model an applied pull through a stretched band.
7. Interpret relative 2 cm and 4 cm meter readings.
8. Diagnose fair and unfair pull comparisons.
9. Read the 80/150 cm result table or graph.
10. Build a complete object-pair, direction and evidence explanation.
